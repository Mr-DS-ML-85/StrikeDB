## 1. Qdrant vs Milvus: Quantization Approaches

**Claim** | Milvus supports scalar quantization (INT8), product quantization (PQ with configurable subquantizers), binary quantization, and IVF-based index types with quantization variants (IVFPQ, IVFSQ). It uses FAISS/Knowhere under the hood for index implementations. | Source: https://github.com/milvus-io/milvus (README) | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant supports four quantization methods: TurboQuant (v1.18+, up to 32x compression), Scalar Quantization (INT8, 4x), Binary Quantization (1/1.5/2-bit, up to 32x), and Product Quantization (up to 64x). Qdrant's TurboQuant is a rotation-based method from Google Research that applies fast Hadamard rotation before compression, enabling uniform codebook application across any vector distribution. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/documentation/manage-data/quantization/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Milvus quantization is primarily based on FAISS implementations (PQ, IVF+PQ, IVF+SQ8), which are well-established but require per-dataset training/calibration to learn codebooks. Qdrant's TurboQuant requires no per-dataset training - the codebook is derived once from the standard normal distribution and is universal across all datasets. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's 4-bit TurboQuant at 8x compression delivers recall within ~1-2 percentage points of scalar quantization (4x compression) on most datasets, and sometimes beats SQ by up to 4.6 pp on specific embeddings (e.g., dbpedia-openai-ada). This means TurboQuant achieves comparable recall to SQ at half the storage. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ (detailed benchmarks table) | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's 2-bit TurboQuant beats Binary Quantization 2-bit by 9-24 percentage points at the same 16x storage class across all ten benchmarked datasets. The largest margins are on laion (+24.0 pp) and h&m (+21.8 pp). | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ (detailed benchmarks table) | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Milvus is faster at indexing time than Qdrant and other engines, but does not match Qdrant's RPS or latency at higher dimension embeddings or larger vector counts. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/ (Qdrant's open-source benchmark, updated Jan/Jun 2024) | Confidence: medium (Qdrant-authored benchmark, may have configuration bias)

**Claim** | Milvus is written in Go + C++ with Knowhere as its vector search engine layer, while Qdrant is written entirely in Rust. Milvus uses a distributed architecture with separate microservices (proxy, query nodes, data nodes, index nodes) and requires etcd + MinIO/S3 for operation. | Source: https://github.com/milvus-io/milvus | Confidence: high

## 2. Qdrant vs pgvector: Quantization and HNSW Differences

**Claim** | pgvector supports HNSW and IVFFlat index types. For quantization, it offers half-precision vectors (`halfvec`, 2x compression), binary quantization (via expression indexing with `binary_quantize()`), and binary vectors (`bit` type). It does NOT support scalar quantization, product quantization, or TurboQuant-style rotation-based quantization. | Source: https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector (README) | Confidence: high

**Claim** | pgvector's HNSW implementation supports configurable `m` (default 16), `ef_construction` (default 64), and `ef_search` (default 40). It also supports iterative index scans for filtered queries (added in v0.8.0) with strict and relaxed ordering modes. pgvector limits vector dimensions: 2,000 for `vector`, 4,000 for `halfvec`, 64,000 for `bit`. | Source: https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector (README) | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's HNSW is a native implementation in Rust with custom segment-based architecture. Unlike pgvector which delegates to Postgres' shared buffer and WAL system, Qdrant manages its own memory and disk I/O with io_uring-based async scoring for disk-based workloads. Qdrant supports unbounded vector dimensions (no hard cap like pgvector's 2000/4000). | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/why-rust/; https://qdrant.tech/articles/io_uring/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | pgvector's binary quantization requires a two-step process: (1) search with binary-quantized vectors, then (2) re-rank with original vectors for better recall. This is a manual workflow. Qdrant's quantization is integrated into the search pipeline with configurable oversampling and rescoring options, and supports asymmetric quantization (different encoding for stored vectors vs queries) natively. | Source: https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector; https://qdrant.tech/documentation/manage-data/quantization/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | pgvector has 22.2k GitHub stars vs Qdrant's ~23k stars (as of July 2026). pgvector's strength is its integration with the PostgreSQL ecosystem (ACID, JOINs, transactions), while Qdrant is purpose-built for vector search performance. | Source: GitHub repositories | Confidence: high

**Claim** | pgvector filtering uses Postgres-native approaches: B-tree indexes on filter columns, partial indexes, partitioning, and iterative index scans. Qdrant implements a custom "Filterable HNSW" approach that avoids both pre-filtering (binary mask) and post-filtering (search then filter), instead integrating filtering directly into the HNSW graph traversal with query planning. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/filterable-hnsw/; https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/ | Confidence: high

## 3. Qdrant vs Weaviate: Quantization Features

**Claim** | Weaviate supports PQ (Product Quantization) with configurable segments, BQ (Binary Quantization), and SQ (Scalar Quantization) as of recent versions. Weaviate also has PQ compression for both HNSW and flat indexes. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/ (comparison notes); Weaviate documentation | Confidence: medium

**Claim** | In Qdrant's open-source vector-db-benchmark, Weaviate showed the least improvement compared to previous runs. Qdrant achieved highest RPS and lowest latencies in almost all scenarios across the tested datasets. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/ | Confidence: medium (Qdrant-authored benchmark)

**Claim** | Weaviate's HNSW implementation is in Go and is part of a larger system that includes schema management, class-based data modeling, and built-in vectorization modules. Weaviate's architecture is more opinionated about data modeling (classes, properties) compared to Qdrant's more flexible payload-based approach. | Source: https://github.com/weaviate/weaviate; Weaviate documentation | Confidence: medium

**Claim** | Weaviate does not have an equivalent to Qdrant's TurboQuant rotation-based quantization. Weaviate's PQ is standard product quantization requiring training, while Qdrant's TurboQuant is training-free with universal codebooks. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: medium (inference from feature comparison)

## 4. Qdrant vs Pinecone: ANN Quality Comparison

**Claim** | Pinecone is a closed-source managed SaaS platform. It does not expose its internal quantization or indexing details publicly, making direct algorithmic comparison difficult. Pinecone uses proprietary ANN implementations optimized for their managed infrastructure. | Source: General knowledge; Pinecone documentation | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's open-source benchmarks specifically exclude closed-source SaaS platforms (including Pinecone) because they cannot be tested on the same hardware, making fair comparison impossible. Qdrant's benchmark philosophy requires reproducible, open-source testing on identical machines. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/ (FAQ section) | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Pinecone offers serverless and pod-based deployment models with automatic scaling. Qdrant offers similar cloud deployment (Qdrant Cloud) plus self-hosted options. Both support filtered search, metadata storage, and hybrid search capabilities. | Source: Pinecone and Qdrant documentation | Confidence: medium

**Claim** | Independent benchmarks (ann-benchmarks, VIBE) test Pinecone in some configurations, but results vary significantly by dataset and query pattern. Qdrant is included in ann-benchmarks as a library-level implementation. | Source: https://github.com/erikbern/ann-benchmarks | Confidence: medium

## 5. Qdrant's Architecture Advantages: Rust, Single-Node Performance

**Claim** | Qdrant is written in Rust, providing memory safety without garbage collector overhead. The Docker container is approximately 50MB. Rust's ownership model eliminates data races at compile time, enabling safe parallelism. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/why-rust/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant chose Rust over Go, Java, C++, and Python because: Java/Scala have GC pauses causing latency spikes; Go's apparent simplicity hides complexity for systems programming; Python is too slow for core search operations; C++ was rejected due to safety concerns at scale. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/why-rust/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's SIMD-optimized scoring kernels for TurboQuant use AVX-VNNI/AVX-512 (x86) and SDOT (ARM) instructions. The 4-bit kernel collapses to: `pshufb` for codebook lookup, `maddubs` + `madd_epi16` for multiply-accumulate, fitting the scoring hot path into a handful of integer SIMD instructions per 16-dimension chunk. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant achieves highest RPS and lowest latencies in almost all single-node benchmark scenarios tested on Azure Standard D8s v3 (8 vCPU, 32 GiB), including a 4x RPS improvement on one dataset compared to competitors. Tests were run on dbpedia-openai-1M (1536d), deep-image-96 (10M vectors, 96d), gist-960 (1M, 960d), and glove-100 (1.2M, 100d). | Source: https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/ | Confidence: medium (Qdrant-authored benchmark)

**Claim** | Qdrant vs Elastic (July 2026 benchmark): At recall@100 ~0.96 on wiki_dpr_e5 (21M vectors, 768d), Qdrant with TurboQuant 4-bit achieved 2.1x higher throughput (67.2 QPS vs 32.4 QPS), 51% lower latency (59.5ms vs 122.6ms), using 1/3 the per-node CPU and RAM (2 vCPU / 8 GB vs 7 vCPU / 26 GB). | Source: https://qdrant.tech/blog/benchmark-elastic-diskbbq/ | Confidence: medium (Qdrant-authored, but with reproducible kit on GitHub)

**Claim** | Qdrant's segment-based architecture allows per-segment calibration for TurboQuant (anisotropy compensation), and the asymmetric scoring scheme means calibration metadata is computed once per segment and applied free at query time. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant uses io_uring for async disk I/O in its two-stage retrieval pipeline: prefetch quantized candidates from RAM, then rescore against on-disk original vectors asynchronously. This is particularly effective for disk-based vector search at scale. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/io_uring/ | Confidence: high

## 8. Qdrant's HNSW Implementation vs hnswlib

**Claim** | hnswlib (part of the nmslib project) is a standalone C++ HNSW library used as a building block by other systems. It focuses purely on the ANN index algorithm without any database features (CRUD, filtering, persistence, networking). | Source: https://github.com/nmslib/hnsw; https://github.com/erikbern/ann-benchmarks | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant implements its own HNSW from scratch in Rust, not wrapping hnswlib. Qdrant's HNSW is deeply integrated with its segment architecture, supporting filterable search (Filterable HNSW), per-segment quantized representations, and async disk-based rescoring. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/filterable-hnsw/; Qdrant documentation | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's Filterable HNSW differs from standard HNSW (including hnswlib) by integrating payload-based filtering directly into the graph traversal algorithm. Instead of post-filtering (search then filter, which can lose results) or pre-filtering (binary mask, which doesn't scale), Qdrant modifies the HNSW search to account for filters during graph traversal, avoiding accuracy collapse. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/filterable-hnsw/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's HNSW supports quantized vector search natively: during graph construction and search, it can use quantized vectors for distance computation while maintaining full-precision vectors for rescoring. This tight integration is not possible with standalone hnswlib. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/documentation/manage-data/quantization/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant implements an advanced query planning strategy that can bypass the vector index entirely when filters are restrictive enough, providing a "speed boost" for highly selective filters. This query planning is specific to Qdrant's HNSW implementation. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/ | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Both Qdrant's HNSW and hnswlib support configurable `m` and `ef_construction`/`ef_search` parameters. Qdrant defaults to `m=16`, `ef_construct=128` in benchmarks; hnswlib defaults to `M=16`, `efConstruction=200`. | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/; hnswlib documentation | Confidence: high

**Claim** | Qdrant's HNSW graph construction supports concurrent operations and is designed for dynamic workloads with real-time insertions and deletions, whereas hnswlib is primarily designed for static or batch-loading scenarios. | Source: Qdrant documentation; hnswlib repository | Confidence: medium
