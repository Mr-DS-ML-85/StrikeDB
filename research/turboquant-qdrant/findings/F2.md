## 1. How Qdrant's TurboQuant Differs from the Paper

Qdrant ships a **hybrid** of TurboQuant and RaBitQ, not a vanilla TurboQuant implementation. They deliberately chose the **MSE variant** over the PROD variant from the paper, and added several production extensions.

**Key deviations from the paper:**

- **MSE variant, not PROD.** The paper's PROD variant spends 1 bit on a QJL random projection for unbiased scoring. Qdrant rejects this because: (a) PROD is asymmetric-only and cannot serve both HNSW build (symmetric scoring) and user search (asymmetric scoring) with one artifact; (b) PROD requires an extra O(D log D) QJL projection per query; (c) PROD splits the bit budget, giving 1 fewer bit to the codebook.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 3 | Confidence: high

- **Length renormalization borrowed from RaBitQ, not from the paper.** Qdrant stores one extra 4-byte scalar per vector (the centroid-norm ratio `l2/cn`) to correct Lloyd-Max shrinkage. The paper's PROD variant solves the same problem via QJL. RaBitQ-style renormalization is simpler and more effective.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 5 | Confidence: high

- **Per-coordinate anisotropy compensation (Qdrant's own extension).** The paper assumes isotropic data (uniform on unit sphere). Real embeddings are anisotropic. Qdrant does a per-segment pre-pass to estimate `(shift, scale)` per coordinate, then offloads the compensation onto the query side at scoring time. This is free at search time for asymmetric scoring.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 7 | Confidence: high

- **1-bit path is pure RaBitQ.** At 1 bit, the TurboQuant paper adds nothing beyond the 2-centroid Lloyd-Max codebook. The dense sign-bit packing and bit-plane AND+popcount scoring are taken directly from RaBitQ (Gao & Long, SIGMOD 2024).
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 10, Conclusion | Confidence: high

- **L2 and unnormalized dot support.** The paper only considers cosine on the unit sphere. Qdrant extends to L2 and dot-product by storing the original L2 norm (+4 bytes/vector) and reconstructing distances via `||q - v||^2 = ||q||^2 + ||v||^2 - 2<q,v>`.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ Section "L2 and Unnormalized Dot" | Confidence: high

- **P-Square quantile calibration instead of mean+stddev.** Qdrant uses the P-Square online quantile estimator (Jain & Chlamtac, 1985) on reservoir-sampled data to estimate shift+scale, rather than assuming Gaussian distribution.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 8 | Confidence: high

**Recall improvement from each extension (vanilla TurboQuant vs Qdrant's implementation, 4-bit, recall@10 on 100K brute-force):**

## 3. How They Combine TurboQuant with HNSW

### Two Scoring Paths, One Artifact

Qdrant maintains **two equally important scoring paths** that share a single quantized artifact on disk:

**Asymmetric path (user search):** The query vector is NOT quantized via TurboQuant. The original float32 query arrives through the API, gets the same Hadamard rotation applied, and scores against stored quantized vectors via a plain dot-product between the continuous rotated query and the codebook indices. This is the path users see.

**Symmetric path (HNSW build, segment merges, internal clustering, rescoring):** Both sides are quantized. The scoring formula is `c[idx_x]` vs `c[idx_y]` -- a dot-product between two centroid-index lookups. This runs during HNSW graph construction and internal operations.

| Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 3 "Asymmetric and symmetric scoring" | Confidence: high

### HNSW Integration Details

- **HNSW graph is built via symmetric scoring.** During index construction, TurboQuant scores between pairs of quantized vectors determine which edges exist in the HNSW graph. The graph is optimized for proximity in "EC-space" (encoding-compensated space), not the original space.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 7 "Symmetric scoring" | Confidence: high

- **At query time, the graph is traversed using asymmetric scoring.** The user's float32 query is rotated and scored against quantized neighbors. The top-K candidates surfaced by graph traversal are then re-scored via the asymmetric path where anisotropy compensation is applied honestly.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 7 | Confidence: high

- **Anisotropy compensation is deliberately NOT applied to symmetric scoring.** In the symmetric path, applying the per-coord `D'^2` weighting would amplify quantization noise from both sides, making scores effectively random. So for symmetric scoring, they compute a plain uniform-weight dot -- a different metric (similarity in EC-space) but fully self-consistent because the HNSW graph is also built in that space.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 7 "Symmetric scoring: we deliberately don't apply the compensation" | Confidence: high

- **They do NOT navigate with cheap distance and rerank with Turbo score** in the traditional sense. Instead, both navigation AND final scoring use TurboQuant scoring -- but through two different paths (symmetric for navigation/build, asymmetric for final scoring). The asymmetric path is strictly more accurate because quantization noise lives on only one side.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Sections 3, 7 | Confidence: high

## 5. Hadamard Rotation in Practice

### Decomposition into Power-of-Two Blocks (No Padding to Nearest Power-of-2)

Qdrant does **NOT** pad dimensions to the nearest power of 2. Instead:

- **Greedy decomposition:** They decompose the dimension as a sum of powers of two. Example: D=300 -> [256, 32, 8, 4]. Each block goes through its own Walsh-Hadamard Transform (WHT) with normalization `1/sqrt(block_size)`.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 6 "Decomposition into power-of-two blocks" | Confidence: high

### Multi-Round Rotation

- **3 rounds of (permute + WHT)** after the initial WHT = 4 WHT applications and 3 permutations total.
- Empirically sufficient: per-coordinate stddev after rotation drops by ~500x compared to original on highly non-uniform input.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 6 "Why one round isn't enough" | Confidence: high

### Pure, Reversible, Non-Persistent Rotation

- **Reversible LCG + Fisher-Yates:** The permutation is implemented as a deterministic Fisher-Yates shuffle driven by a reversible Linear Congruential Generator (Knuth MMIX constants: A=6364136223846793005, C=1442695040888963407).
- **O(1) state:** The entire permutation is described by 3 64-bit integers (seed, count, end_state), independent of dimension.
- **Not persisted to disk:** The rotation is fully described by the dimension plus hardcoded constants in code. No rotation matrix stored.
- **Fully reversible:** The backward LCG step (`A_INV * (state - C) mod 2^64`) is as cheap as the forward step.
- **Bug caught:** Must use upper 32 bits of LCG state for modulo, not lower bits. Lower bits have short periods and produce degenerate permutations (for D=4, only 12 of 24 permutations covered instead of all).
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 6 | Confidence: high

## 6. RAM Usage Claims Per Vector at Each Bit-Width

Qdrant does not publish explicit per-vector RAM usage numbers for TurboQuant. However, the storage layout is well-documented:

### Storage per Vector

| Component | Size | Notes |
|-----------|------|-------|
| Quantized indices | b * D bits | b = bit width (1, 1.5, 2, or 4) |
| L2 norm (original length) | 4 bytes (float32) | Required for L2 and dot-product metrics; also used for renormalization |
| Per-coord shift+scale metadata | Stored per segment, not per vector | ~2 * D * 8 bytes per segment (for P-Square calibration) |

### Concrete RAM per Vector (1536-dim embedding)

| Encoding | Compression | Storage per vector (approx) |
|----------|-------------|----------------------------|
| bits4 (default) | 8x | 1536 * 4 bits = 768 bytes + 4 bytes norm = **772 bytes** |
| bits2 | 16x | 1536 * 2 bits = 384 bytes + 4 bytes norm = **388 bytes** |
| bits1_5 | 24x | 1536 * 1.5 bits = 288 bytes + 4 bytes norm = **292 bytes** |
| bits1 | 32x | 1536 * 1 bit = 192 bytes + 4 bytes norm = **196 bytes** |

Compare to original float32: 1536 * 4 = **6144 bytes** per vector.

| Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ + https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 9 | Confidence: high (derived from documented layout)

### Pre-pass Memory (Temporary, During Indexing Only)

- Reservoir sampling stores `R * D * 8` bytes per segment. At D=1024, R=8192: **~67 MB**. At D=3072: **~200 MB**.
- Plus chunk buffers (~32 MB at D=1024).
- All freed after calibration completes.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 8 "Memory is not free here" | Confidence: high

## 7. Known Limitations and Bugs

### Documented Limitations

- **L1 (Manhattan) distance is NOT supported efficiently.** Rotation does not preserve L1 norm. L1 scoring requires full dequantization + inverse rotation per comparison (O(D log D) per score), which defeats the speedup. L1 is described as "an emergency fallback" and "not recommended for production loads."
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 9 "What about L1?" | Confidence: high

- **Symmetric scoring does not apply anisotropy compensation.** The HNSW graph is built in EC-space (encoding-compensated space), not the original space. This is a deliberate design choice, but means the graph topology is optimized for a different metric than the final asymmetric scoring.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 7 | Confidence: high

- **MSE variant has known shrinkage at low bit widths.** At b=1, MSE loses ~36% of every dot-product. At b=2, ~12%. This is a uniform scalar that doesn't affect ranking, and renormalization corrects for it, but it's a fundamental property of the MSE variant.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 3 "Honest about the trade-off" | Confidence: high

- **1-bit with anisotropy compensation requires 12-bit query quantization.** At 1-bit storage with compensation enabled, the query must be widened from 8 to 12 bits to avoid rounding noise on squashed coordinates. Without this fix, recall sags noticeably.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 7 "The 1-bit metric" | Confidence: high

- **Per-segment calibration overhead.** The P-Square calibration pre-pass runs per segment and is the most expensive stage of the build. Memory budget is independent of dataset size (only dim and R drive it). At high dimensions (D=3072) the temporary memory can reach ~200 MB.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 8 | Confidence: high

- **LCG lower-bits bug was caught and fixed.** Using lower 32 bits of LCG state for Fisher-Yates modulo produces degenerate permutations. Must use upper 32 bits. This was a real bug found during development.
  | Source: https://ivanpleshkov.dev/blog/turboquant/ Section 6 | Confidence: high

### No Documented Bugs in v1.18.0 Release Notes

The v1.18.0 release notes do not mention any TurboQuant-specific bugs. The bug fixes in v1.18.0 are unrelated to quantization (stop words, datetime parsing, IsEmpty condition, geo circle checks, HNSW sub graph infinite loop, etc.).
  | Source: https://github.com/qdrant/qdrant/releases/tag/v1.18.0 | Confidence: high

## 8. Comparison vs Product Quantization (PQ) and INT8 (Scalar Quantization)

### TurboQuant vs Scalar Quantization (INT8, 4x compression)

- **TQ 4-bit (8x) is competitive with SQ (4x) at half the storage.** On 9 of 10 datasets the gap to SQ is within 2pp in either direction. On 3 datasets (dbp-oai, cohere, laion) TQ 4-bit actually beats SQ by up to 4.6pp.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: high

- **When SQ's int8 grid is mismatched with the embedding distribution, TQ 4-bit does better despite using half the bits.** The anisotropy compensation in TQ adapts to the real distribution, while SQ uses a uniform int8 grid.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ "Detailed Benchmarks" | Confidence: high

- **Qdrant's recommendation:** "Unless you need to use the Manhattan (L1) distance metric, consider using 4-bit TurboQuant instead of scalar quantization, as it offers comparable recall at double the compression."
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/documentation/manage-data/quantization/ "How to Choose" | Confidence: high

### TurboQuant vs Binary Quantization (BQ)

- **TQ 2-bit beats BQ 2-bit by 9-24pp** at the same 16x storage class. Largest margins on laion (+24pp) and h&m (+21.8pp).
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: high

- **TQ 1-bit beats vanilla BQ 1-bit by 9-21pp** at 32x. Against asymmetric BQ (1-bit storage, 8-bit query), TQ 1-bit is still ahead by 0.1-10pp.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: high

- **BQ is faster but TQ has better recall.** Qdrant's official guidance: "At 16x: Binary quantization is faster, but TurboQuant provides better recall." Same pattern at 24x and 32x.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/documentation/manage-data/quantization/ "How to Choose" | Confidence: high

- **BQ requires centered distributions.** TurboQuant overcomes this limitation via the Hadamard rotation + per-coord calibration, making it work with any vector distribution.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ | Confidence: high

### TurboQuant vs Product Quantization (PQ)

- **Qdrant does NOT directly benchmark TurboQuant vs PQ.** PQ is described as useful for "up to 64x compression when minimizing memory is the top priority." TurboQuant tops out at 32x.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/documentation/manage-data/quantization/ | Confidence: high

- **PQ requires per-dataset codebook training; TurboQuant does not.** This is a fundamental architectural difference. PQ needs k-means clustering on representative data to learn 256 centroids per sub-vector. TurboQuant uses a universal Lloyd-Max codebook derived once from N(0,1).
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ "What Is TurboQuant?" | Confidence: high

- **PQ distance calculations are not SIMD-friendly; TQ is.** PQ requires asymmetric distance computation through sub-codebooks. TQ's 4-bit/2-bit kernels are a stream of integer multiply-adds (pshufb + maddubs + madd_epi16), near-perfect for AVX-VNNI/AVX-512/NEON.
  | Source: https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/ + https://qdrant.tech/documentation/manage-data/quantization/ | Confidence: high

- **The Google Research paper shows TurboQuant achieving superior recall ratios vs PQ** on GloVe dataset (d=200) at various k values, despite PQ using "inefficient large codebooks and dataset-specific tuning."
  | Source: https://research.google/blog/turboquant-redefining-ai-efficiency-with-extreme-compression/ | Confidence: medium (Google's own benchmarks, not independently verified)

## Summary: Key Takeaways for DB-Strike

1. **Qdrant's TurboQuant is not vanilla TurboQuant.** It's a hybrid of TurboQuant (MSE codebook, integer SIMD kernels) + RaBitQ (length renormalization, 1-bit scoring) + their own anisotropy compensation. Any fair comparison must account for these extensions.

2. **No QPS/latency benchmarks published.** Qdrant focuses entirely on recall comparisons. Throughput claims are implicit ("SIMD-optimized integer scoring") rather than measured.

3. **HNSW integration is NOT "navigate cheap, rerank Turbo."** Both navigation and final scoring use TurboQuant -- symmetric scoring for HNSW build, asymmetric for user queries.

4. **The biggest recall wins come from RaBitQ renormalization (+9-15pp on aniso data) and anisotropy compensation (+1.5-8.8pp).** The rotation and codebook are standard TurboQuant.

5. **Hadamard rotation does NOT pad to power-of-2.** It decomposes dimensions into sum-of-powers-of-two blocks, which is more memory-efficient.

6. **RAM per vector is well-defined:** b*D bits + 4 bytes norm. For 1536-dim: 772B (4-bit), 388B (2-bit), 292B (1.5-bit), 196B (1-bit).

7. **No known TurboQuant-specific bugs in production.** The LCG lower-bits bug was caught pre-release.

8. **L1 metric is the only hard limitation.** All other metrics (cosine, dot, L2) are first-class.
