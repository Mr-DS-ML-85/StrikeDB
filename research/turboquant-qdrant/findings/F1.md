## 1. The Exact Algorithm: Two-Stage TurboQuant

**Claim** | TurboQuant uses a two-stage process: (1) random rotation + (b-1)-bit Lloyd-Max scalar quantization on each coordinate, then (2) 1-bit QJL (Quantized Johnson-Lindenstrauss) transform on the residual for unbiased inner product estimation. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 3 (TurboQuant: High Performance Quantization) | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | Stage 1 (TurboQuant_mse): Input vector x on unit sphere is rotated via random rotation matrix Θ (d×d, generated via QR decomposition of random Gaussian matrix). The rotated vector Θ·x has each coordinate following a Beta distribution: f_X(x) = Γ(d/2)/(π·Γ((d-1)/2)) · (1-x²)^((d-3)/2). Lloyd-Max algorithm finds optimal 2^b centroids for this distribution. Each coordinate is quantized to nearest centroid index (b-bit integer). Dequantization: retrieve centroids, then rotate back via Θ^T. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Algorithm 1, Section 3.1 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | Stage 2 (TurboQuant_prod): Apply TurboQuant_mse with (b-1) bits to get quantized reconstruction. Compute residual r = x - DeQuant_mse(Quant_mse(x)). Apply QJL to residual: sign(S·r) where S is d×d random Gaussian matrix. Store (b-1)-bit indices + d-bit QJL sign vector + ||r||₂ scalar. Dequantization combines MSE reconstruction with QJL residual correction. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Algorithm 2, Section 3.2 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | The QJL map is defined as Q_qjl(x) = sign(S·x) for S ∈ ℝ^{d×d} with i.i.d. N(0,1) entries. Dequantization: Q_qjl^{-1}(z) = (π/(2d)) · S^T · z. This provides an unbiased inner product estimator with variance ≤ (π²/d)·||y||₂². | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Definition 1, Lemma 4 | Confidence: **high**

## 2. Theoretical Guarantees

**Claim** | TurboQuant_mse achieves MSE distortion D_mse ≤ 3π² · (1/4^b) for any bit-width b ≥ 0 and unit-norm vector x. For small bit-widths: b=1 → 0.36, b=2 → 0.117, b=3 → 0.03, b=4 → 0.009. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Theorem 1 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | TurboQuant_prod is provably unbiased: E[⟨y, Q_prod^{-1}(Q_prod(x))⟩] = ⟨y, x⟩ for all y, x. Inner product distortion: D_prod ≤ 3π² · (||y||₂²/d) · (1/4^b). For small bit-widths: b=1 → 1.57/d, b=2 → 0.56/d, b=3 → 0.18/d, b=4 → 0.047/d. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Theorem 2 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | Information-theoretic lower bounds (via Shannon's lower bound + Yao's minimax): D_mse(Q) ≥ 1/4^b and D_prod(Q) ≥ (||y||₂²/d) · (1/4^b) for any randomized quantizer. TurboQuant is within a factor of 3π² ≈ 2.7 of these bounds asymptotically, and only ~1.45× at b=1. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Theorem 3 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | The proof uses Lemma 1 (coordinate distribution of random point on hypersphere follows Beta distribution), Lemma 2 (Shannon Lower Bound for MSE), and Lemma 3 (SLB specialized to uniform distribution on unit hypersphere: D(B) ≥ 2^{-2B/d}). | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 2 | Confidence: **high**

## 3. Why "Data-Oblivious"

**Claim** | TurboQuant is data-oblivious because the codebook is derived analytically from the Beta distribution of rotated coordinates, not learned from data. After random rotation, each coordinate of Θ·x follows a known Beta distribution regardless of the input vector x. The Lloyd-Max centroids for this distribution are precomputed once and stored. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 3.1: "We solve [the continuous k-means] for a range of practically relevant bit-widths b once, and store the results for future uses by the quantizer." | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | The key insight enabling data-obliviousness is that in high dimensions d, the Beta distribution converges to N(0, 1/d), and distinct coordinates become nearly independent (by concentration of measure). This means optimal scalar quantizers for the Beta distribution are near-optimal for the vector quantization problem, without needing any knowledge of the input data distribution. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 1.3: "Furthermore, any two distinct coordinates become nearly uncorrelated and, more importantly, almost independent" | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | For moderate dimensions, the optimal quantization centroids for b=1 are {±2/√(πd)} and for b=2 are {±0.453/√d, ±1.51/√d}, derived analytically from the Beta/normal approximation. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 3.1 | Confidence: **high**

## 4. SRHT (Subsampled Randomized Hadamard Transform) Trick

**Claim** | The paper uses a full random rotation matrix Θ generated via QR decomposition of a random Gaussian matrix (O(d³) time) and a full random projection matrix S with i.i.d. N(0,1) entries (O(d²) per vector). The paper does NOT explicitly use SRHT or Walsh-Hadamard Transform in its theoretical analysis or pseudocode. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Algorithm 1 line 2, Algorithm 2 line 3 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | In practice, the Walsh-Hadamard Transform (WHT) is a standard known trick to approximate random orthogonal rotation in O(d log d) instead of O(d²), which would be used in any practical implementation. This is NOT unique to TurboQuant - it's a general technique used across many quantization algorithm implementations. | Source: Common knowledge in quantization literature; paper acknowledges practical acceleration without detailing SRHT specifically | Confidence: **medium** (SRHT not explicitly discussed in the paper, but is the standard practical optimization)

**Claim** | The QJL computation sign(S·r) with S ∈ ℝ^{d×d} has O(d²) complexity per vector in the naive implementation. In practice, SRHT-based approximations can reduce this to O(d log d), similar to how the random rotation Θ can be approximated. | Source: Inferred from standard JL/sketching literature and the paper's emphasis on accelerator-friendliness | Confidence: **medium** (not explicitly analyzed in the paper, but is the standard practical optimization)

## 5. Bit Packing: Code Bits + Residual Norm + QJL Bits

**Claim** | For TurboQuant_prod with b-bit total budget, the per-vector storage consists of: (1) (b-1)·d bits for the MSE codebook indices (one (b-1)-bit index per coordinate), (2) d bits for the QJL sign vector ({-1,+1}^d, stored as 1 bit per entry), and (3) a floating-point scalar ||r||₂ (the residual norm, ~32 bits). Total: approximately b·d + 32 bits per vector. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Algorithm 2, output: (idx, qjl, ||r||₂) | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | The residual norm ||r||₂ is stored in floating-point precision as overhead (one scalar per vector). For d=1536 and b=4, this is 4·1536 + 32 = 6176 bits ≈ 772 bytes per vector, vs 1536·32 = 49152 bytes for f32, giving ~63.6× compression. The overhead of the norm is negligible at ~0.5%. | Source: Computed from Algorithm 2 output specification | Confidence: **medium** (compression ratio computed, not directly stated in paper)

**Claim** | For TurboQuant_mse (without QJL correction), the storage is simply b·d bits (one b-bit index per coordinate), with no residual norm needed. Dequantization only requires the stored indices and the precomputed codebook + rotation matrix. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Algorithm 1 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | The random rotation matrix Θ and QJL projection matrix S are shared/global parameters, not stored per-vector. They are generated once during setup and used for all vectors. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Algorithm 1 line 2-3, Algorithm 2 line 2-3 | Confidence: **high**

## 6. Compression Ratios

**Claim** | Bit-width modes and compression vs f32 (32 bits/coordinate): b=4 → 8× compression (4 bits/coord), b=2 → 16× compression (2 bits/coord), b=1.5 → 21.3× compression (not a standard mode, but achievable with entropy coding reducing b=2 effective rate), b=1 → 32× compression (1 bit/coord). | Source: Derived from paper's bit-width definition (B = b·d total bits for d-dimensional vector) | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | The paper demonstrates KV cache compression exceeding 5× (at 3.5 bits effective) while maintaining quality parity with full precision. At 2.5 bits, marginal quality degradation occurs. The effective non-integer bit-widths (2.5, 3.5) are achieved by splitting channels into outlier (higher precision) and regular (lower precision) sets with independent TurboQuant instances. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.3, Table 1 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | Entropy encoding of codebook pointers could reduce average bit-width by ~5% at b=4 (entropy of codeword distribution ≈ 3.8 bits vs 4 bits nominal). The paper chose not to incorporate this for simplicity. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 3.1, "Entropy Encoding Codebook Pointers" | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | Quantization time comparison at 4-bit: TurboQuant 0.0007-0.0021s vs PQ 37-494s vs RabitQ 597-3957s across d=200 to d=3072. TurboQuant indexing time is essentially zero. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Table 2 | Confidence: **high**

## 7. Comparison to Product Quantization (PQ)

**Claim** | TurboQuant outperforms PQ in recall ratio across all experiments (GloVe d=200, OpenAI3 d=1536, OpenAI3 d=3072), despite PQ being given data-dependent advantages (training and evaluation on same dataset). | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.4, Figure 5 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | PQ requires k-means codebook construction during indexing (heavy preprocessing), while TurboQuant has zero indexing time. PQ codebook size grows exponentially with bits, adding storage overhead. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.4 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | PQ using LUT16 (16 codewords) showed substantial quality degradation; LUT256 (256 codewords) was needed for acceptable accuracy, but this increases memory usage. For 2-bit PQ, 4 coordinates are grouped per lookup; for 4-bit, 2 coordinates per lookup. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.4 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | RabitQ lacks fully vectorized implementation, cannot leverage GPU acceleration, runs significantly slower on CPU, and uses more bits than reported due to computational overheads. TurboQuant outperforms RabitQ in recall. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.4 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | TurboQuant is accelerator-friendly (vectorizable, GPU-compatible) due to its reliance on matrix multiplications (rotation, QJL projection) and simple per-coordinate operations (nearest centroid lookup), unlike PQ's binary search or RabitQ's non-vectorized approach. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 1, 1.2 | Confidence: **high**

## 8. Benchmarks vs Other Methods

**Claim** | Needle-in-a-Haystack (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct): TurboQuant achieves score 0.997 = identical to full-precision baseline, at 4× compression. Comparison: SnapKV 0.858, PyramidKV 0.895, KIVI 0.981, PolarQuant 0.995. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.2, Figure 4 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | LongBench (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct): TurboQuant at 2.5 bits achieves average 49.44 (vs full cache 50.06). At 3.5 bits achieves 50.06 = matches full precision exactly. KIVI at 3 bits: 48.50. PolarQuant at 3.9 bits: 49.78. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Table 1 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | LongBench (Ministral-7B-Instruct): TurboQuant at 2.5 bits achieves 49.62 average vs full cache 49.89. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Table 1 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | Nearest neighbor search: TurboQuant consistently outperforms PQ and RabitQ in recall@k across GloVe (d=200), OpenAI3 (d=1536), and OpenAI3 (d=3072) datasets. Quantization time is 100-1000× faster than PQ and 100000×+ faster than RabitQ. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.4, Table 2, Figure 5 | Confidence: **high**

**Claim** | The theoretical distortion bounds are empirically validated on DBpedia Entities dataset (OpenAI3 embeddings, d=1536): observed distortions closely match theoretical predictions across bit-widths, approaching the established lower bounds. | Source: arXiv:2504.19874, Section 4.1, Figure 3 | Confidence: **high**

## Summary of Key Properties

| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Algorithm type | Online / Data-oblivious |
| Two variants | TurboQuant_mse (MSE-optimal), TurboQuant_prod (unbiased inner product) |
| Core technique | Random rotation + Lloyd-Max scalar quantization + QJL residual |
| MSE bound | ≤ 3π² / 4^b (within 2.7× of optimal) |
| Inner product bound | Unbiased; variance ≤ 3π² · ||y||₂² / (d · 4^b) |
| Indexing time | ~0 (vs minutes for PQ/RabitQ) |
| GPU compatible | Yes (matrix multiply + per-coord operations) |
| KV cache quality | Matches full precision at 3.5 bits (4.5× compression) |
| NN search | Outperforms PQ/RabitQ in recall at all bit-widths |
